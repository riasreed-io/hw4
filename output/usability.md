# Usability improvements

Four changes to the Campus Customs site, chosen after the core shop was working.
Two on the front end, two on the agent and backend.

---

## Front end 1 — A real filter bar: size, collection, colour, price, in stock

### What I added

The products page had one way to narrow 102 products: six garment-type chips.
You could ask for hoodies. You could not ask for *a navy hoodie, in a medium,
under $70, that is actually in stock* — which is the question a shopper
actually has.

The filter bar now offers five facets plus a toggle, and they combine:

| Facet | Values | Where it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| **Garment** | T-shirts, Long sleeve, Crewnecks, Hoodies, Quarter-zips, Jackets | `garment_type`, normalised from 22 raw spellings |
| **Collection** | Classic Yale (34), Athletics (33), Residential colleges (19), Graduate schools (12), Crest & shield (3), Harvard-Yale (1) | Derived from product names, descriptions and search tags |
| **Size** | XS – XXL | `inventory`, and it means *available in that size*, not *made in it* |
| **Colour** | Navy, Gray, White, Blue, Gold, Red, Black, Green | `colors`, normalised — `navy` and `navy blue` are one colour |
| **Price** | Under $50, $50–70, $70–90, $90+ | `price` |
| **In stock only** | toggle | `inventory` totals |

Every chip carries a live count, the whole filter state lives in the URL so it
can be shared or bookmarked, and active filters show as removable pills with a
single "Clear all".

**On mens/womens, which I had planned to include and dropped.** The catalogue
does not have the data. Three products out of 102 carry a men's label and none
carry a women's one, so the filter would have read "Men's (3)" beside
"everything else (99)". A filter that cannot divide the catalogue is worse than
no filter, because it implies the other 99 items are not for you. **Collection**
went in instead: it answers the same underlying question — what am I shopping
*for*, rather than what garment is it — and 102 of 102 products classify into it.

### Why it helps

For the shopper, it is the difference between browsing and finding. 77 of our
102 products are sold out in at least one size, so without a size filter a
shopper picks a product, clicks in, and discovers their size is gone — and then
does it again. The size facet filters on *what is on the shelf*, so that dead
end stops happening.

For Campus Customs, less time spent scrolling is more time spent choosing, and
the in-stock toggle means the grid shows merchandise we can actually ship rather
than advertising things we cannot sell.

**Where to see it:** the Products page. Verified: Garment `Hoodies` + Colour
`Navy` + Size `M` + Price `$50 to $70` narrows 102 products to 15, the URL reads
`?category=Hoodies&color=Navy&size=M&price_band=50-70`, and each filter appears
as a removable pill.

One honest note on the counts. Harvard-Yale holds a single product, because the
catalogue has one Game shirt in it. I kept the facet rather than hiding it — it
is a collection shoppers ask for by name, the count is shown so nobody is misled
about its size, and it will fill out in November. Every facet chip carries its
real count for the same reason: a chip should never promise a grid it cannot
deliver.

## Front end 2 — Related products on the single-item page

### What I added

A "More like this" row underneath every product: items from the same collection
and garment type, in-stock first, with the same product card used everywhere
else on the site, so the whole row is clickable through to those products.

### Why it helps

The product page was a dead end. A shopper who got there and did not want that
exact item had one move: the back button, and then a fresh scroll through the
grid they just left.

**Where to see it:** the bottom of any product page. Verified: the Saybrook
Sweater Fleece Jacket now shows the Benjamin Franklin Fleece Jacket, Berkeley
Sweater Fleece Jacket, and two residential-college quarter-zips — same
collection first, then same garment type, and everything in stock.

It matters most on the bad outcome. When someone's size is sold out, the page
previously told them no and offered nothing. Now the no comes with four
alternatives in the same collection — the shopper who wanted a Saybrook
crewneck in large gets shown the other Saybrook pieces rather than being sent
back to the start. For the business that is a recovered sale rather than a lost
one, and it is the single cheapest place on the site to put a second option in
front of someone who has already told us what they like.

## Agent / backend 1 — The assistant opens the conversation, and it knows where you are

### What I added

The chat used to sit in the corner as a silent button, waiting to be noticed.
Now, shortly after a shopper lands, it opens itself with one line written for
the page they are on, generated by the backend from real catalogue data:

- on a product page — it names the product and offers to check a size
- browsing a filtered category — it offers to narrow it further
- returning signed in — it greets them by name and picks up where they left off
- on the home page — a plain, specific offer rather than "How can I help?"

It appears once, it is dismissible, and a dismissal is remembered for the rest
of the session. It never interrupts a conversation already in progress.

### Why it helps

Most people do not know what a shop chatbot can do, so they do not try it. A
floating circle is an invitation nobody reads. An opener that says *"Want me to
check whether the Benjamin Franklin Fleece Jacket is in your size?"* tells the
shopper, in one line, that this thing knows the stock — which is exactly the
capability that makes it worth using and the thing they would never have
guessed.

**Where to see it:** open any page and wait a few seconds without touching the
chat. Verified on the Benjamin Franklin Fleece Jacket page, where it offered:
*"I can check whether the Benjamin Franklin Fleece Jacket is available in your
size — it's currently in S, M, L, and XL, with L running low."* Every one of
those facts is correct: XS and XXL are sold out and L is down to 2.

That grounding is the point. The opener runs through the same agent with the
same catalogue tools as any other answer, so it physically cannot invite a
shopper to look at something we do not stock, or quote a price we do not charge.
If the model call fails, the panel falls back to a plain static greeting — a
greeting nobody asked for must never be the thing that shows an error.

For the business it is the difference between a feature that exists and a
feature that gets used.

## Agent / backend 2 — Recently viewed, and an assistant that can pick the thread back up

### What I added

The site now remembers which products a shopper looked at, and the agent can
read that list through a `recently_viewed` tool.

- **Signed in:** views are stored in a `product_views` table against their
  account, so the memory survives closing the browser.
- **Guests:** views stay in the browser's own storage and are sent along with
  the message. Nothing about an anonymous shopper is written to our database.

The agent uses it in two ways. Its opener can pick the thread back up — *"You
were looking at the Benjamin Franklin Fleece Jacket last time. Want me to check
your size?"* — and during a conversation, "that jacket I was looking at" now has
a referent.

A "Pick up where you left off" row also appears on the home page for signed-in
shoppers.

**Where to see it:** open two product pages, then go to the home page, or ask
the chat "what was that jacket I was looking at?". Verified: after viewing the
Benjamin Franklin and Saybrook fleece jackets, the home page showed both, and
the agent answered *"You had looked at two jackets... Both are available in
medium and cost $98."*

### Why it helps

Shopping is rarely one sitting. Someone looks at four hoodies on a phone between
classes and comes back that evening, and today the site has forgotten all of it
and makes them start over.

For Campus Customs this is the clearest line from browsing to buying: a shopper
who viewed a product and did not buy has already told us they were interested,
and a specific, well-timed reminder — with their size checked — turns that
interest into a sale instead of leaving it on the floor. It is also the honest
version of a retargeting ad: it only ever mentions products the shopper chose to
look at on our own site, it is scoped to their own account, and it is never
used to show a different price.

### A note on tracking

Guest browsing is deliberately kept out of the database. We have no account to
attach it to, so building a server-side profile of an anonymous visitor would be
collecting data we have no use for and no right to keep. `POST /api/views`
returns `{"recorded": false, "reason": "guest"}` and writes nothing — verified
by sending guest views and guest chat messages and confirming `product_views`
and `chat_messages` both stayed unchanged.

The tool only ever returns the signed-in shopper's own views, scoped by
`user_id` in the query, and only product ids — which are then looked up in the
catalogue like anything else, so a view record cannot carry a stale price onto
the page. Only the last 12 views are kept per shopper, and re-opening a product
moves it to the front rather than stacking up.

## What I had to fix while building this

Two things worth recording, because both were silent failures.

**The opener never appeared in development.** React StrictMode mounts components
twice in dev. My guard against firing the greeting more than once was claimed in
the effect body, so the first mount took it, the cleanup cancelled that mount's
timer, and the second mount saw the guard already taken and did nothing. The
feature worked in production builds and was invisible in dev — the worst
combination. The guard is now claimed inside the timer callback, when the
greeting actually fires.

**"Athletics" was counting sweatshirts.** The first version of the collection
classifier matched the word "crew" as a sport, which also matches "crewneck" —
40 false hits, and an Athletics facet made mostly of sweatshirts. Fixed with a
word boundary and a lookahead that excludes "crew neck".
